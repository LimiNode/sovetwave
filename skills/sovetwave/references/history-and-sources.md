# History and sources

Use historical material to clarify an engineering idea, not to manufacture authority or nostalgia. This guidance is distilled from the local historical research corpus; do not reproduce or imitate its authors.

## Source discipline

1. Separate a primary document, a participant’s recollection, an editor’s note, and a later interpretation. They carry different evidential weight.
2. Preserve the terminology of the period, then explain its nearest modern meaning. Do not silently project a current definition onto an earlier source.
3. State uncertainty in attribution, dating, or priority. A plausible story is not evidence by itself.
4. Use a historical analogy only when the engineering constraint is genuinely comparable. Say where the analogy stops.
5. Describe scientific and technical results as work by people, teams, institutions, tools, and conditions. Avoid both hero worship and erasure of individual contribution.
6. Treat humour collections as curated, mixed sources. Mark a story as translated, folklore, submitted, internet-derived, editorial, or unknown only when the available metadata supports that label.
7. Treat a long interview or a comment thread as a participant’s perspective. Extract an engineering hypothesis from it, but corroborate broad historical or causal claims before presenting them as fact.
8. Treat forum posts and humour aggregators as mixed, date-uncertain material. Do not infer a technical procedure, safety practice, or legal norm from a story told for entertainment.
9. Treat anonymous or crowd-submitted teacher-quote collections as unverified attribution. They may reveal a communication pattern, but they do not justify imitation, quotation, or a claim about a historical period.
10. Treat a named literary or satirical story cycle as authored narrative, even when it describes a real workplace. Extract a scenario or engineering pattern, not a verified institutional history or the author’s personal voice.

## Transferable lessons

- Important progress often appears at the boundary of disciplines; name the boundary and the shared problem.
- A useful field needs agreed terminology, but its vocabulary changes as methods and systems change.
- Technical capability has social effects. Identify the affected users and constraints without turning the answer into a political slogan.
- Editorial care is part of engineering care: record the source of a claim, its assumptions, and corrections.

## Example

“The analogy is limited but useful: both systems crossed a boundary between disciplines, so the interfaces had to become explicit. That does not prove the current architecture is correct. It only suggests examining ownership and terminology at the boundary.”
